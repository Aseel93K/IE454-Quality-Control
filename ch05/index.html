Is it noise, or is something wrong?

Every process wiggles. A control chart tells the normal wiggle (chance causes) apart from a real problem (an assignable cause), so you fix what is broken and leave the rest alone. Build a chart, catch a shift, read the zone rules, then use the other six tools to find the cause.

It is impossible to inspect or test quality into a product; the product must be built right the first time. Montgomery, Part 3

False alarms and the in-control ARL

Even a perfect process sometimes plots a point outside the limits by chance. That is a type I error, a false alarm. The average run length ARL₀ = 1/α tells you how many samples pass, on average, before one happens.

How fast will the chart catch a shift?

The mean drifts from 1.500 to a new value. β is the chance a sample still plots inside the limits; p = 1 − β is the chance it signals. Then ARL₁ = 1/p and ATS = ARL₁ × h. Bigger samples or more frequent sampling catch shifts sooner.

Book check. For n = 5 and a shift to 1.725, Figure 5.9 gives about 0.35 as the probability of plotting between the limits. That is β, so p = 1 − 0.35 = 0.65 and ARL₁ ≈ 1.5 samples. The book's text uses p = 0.35 (ARL₁ = 2.86), which mixes up β and p. The exact normal calculation here gives β ≈ 0.36.

Western Electric zone rules

Zones C, B and A sit 1, 2 and 3 sigma from the center line. Rule 1: one point beyond 3σ. Rule 2: two of three beyond 2σ. Rule 3: four of five beyond 1σ. Rule 4: eight in a row on one side. Rules 2 to 4 count one side at a time.

More rules, more false alarms

Each extra rule makes the chart more sensitive to small shifts, but every rule can also fire by chance. With k independent rules, the overall false alarm probability is α = 1 − Π(1 − αᵢ).
